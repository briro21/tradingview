Part one. The night math changed. It's a Tuesday evening in New York. Rutgers number theorist Alex Kontorovich is at a gala at the National Museum of Mathematics, a whiskey in one hand and his phone in the other. He keeps refreshing, because OpenAI is about to release something around six o'clock. Then it drops. Seven hundred solved problems. And the second thing he sees is quasi R H, a partial version of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous open problem in his field. His first thought: that can't be what's in this drop. He says he simply could not compute what he was seeing.

At the same gala is MIT's Ankur Moitra. For him it wasn't a total surprise. For weeks there had been rumors that the biggest problems in his area had fallen, including the Unique Games Conjecture. One colleague even rushed out his own paper, with a footnote saying that because of the rumors, he couldn't wait any longer. Just hours before the drop, Ankur asked Subhash Khot, the man who proposed the Unique Games Conjecture, whether he would read an AI proof of it. Khot's answer was simply: no.

And this wasn't even the first shock. A few weeks earlier, Alex gave a talk at Princeton explaining that AI was still years away from formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem in the proof checker Lean. Two days later, Anthropic released exactly that. Thirteen million lines of verified code. As far as Alex understands, two or three people were involved, and most of their input was typing: keep going. Then came Navier-Stokes. For Alex, the quasi Riemann night was round three.
