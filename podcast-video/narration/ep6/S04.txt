Part four. Why mathematicians are mourning. Fields medalist Terence Tao wrote that he is horrified by years of patient, deliberately slow research by graduate students being casually disrupted. You can't unhear a movie spoiler. And then: I mourn the path not taken.

Ankur feels it too. The old blueprint for young mathematicians was simple. Take a problem people care about, make progress on it, and doors open. That blueprint is gone. He says he feels more intellectually lonely than before. Some people now run scripts that scrape the web for open problems and flood the preprint servers with AI papers. And there's a darker side effect. Mathematicians may start hiding their ideas, because once an idea is public, an AI might finish the job in a single night.

Alex tells the story of Andrew Wiles. Years after proving Fermat's Last Theorem, Wiles sat in front of a camera and tried to describe the moment he finally saw how to fix his proof. He said he would never again have a moment that powerful. And he choked up. Could you get that moment by pressing a button? Absolutely not, Alex says. It's like training for years to climb a mountain, and then a helicopter lands and drops you at the top.

Lex compares it to Olympic champions who feel empty after winning gold, and to programmers who felt a loss of meaning when AI started writing most of their code. But not everyone is mourning. Steve is mostly hopeful. If another country invented teleportation, he says, we would reinvent it within five years. Knowing a solution exists is also a huge motivation.
