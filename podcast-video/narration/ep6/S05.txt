Part five. What AI can't take from us. Ankur's answer starts with something called the doorman fallacy. If you think a doorman only opens doors, you replace him with an automatic door. But you lose everything else. The welcome, the recognition, the security, the feeling of being cared for. The visible part of a mathematician's job is proving theorems. But the real job, Ankur says, is thinking deeply. New theories, new connections, new ways of seeing. When problem solving becomes cheap, everything else becomes more valuable.

So what's the one thing a machine still can't give you? Ankur tells it as a story. In the nineteen fifties, a mathematician wrote a play. John von Neumann dies, goes to heaven, and gets to ask God one question. Is the Riemann hypothesis true? God says yes. Everyone exhales. Then von Neumann asks: but how do you know? And God says: I know where all the zeros are, and they're all on the line. That's not enough for von Neumann. He gets so agitated trying to understand why, that he ends up in the other place.

Alex sums it up. Math is not the fact. It's the why. If you tell me something is true and I don't understand why, you have added zero to my knowledge. A proof sitting as ones and zeros in a data center, he argues, is not the value of pure mathematics. The value lives in human heads. That's why he doesn't believe AI destroys mathematics, even in a world where it could solve everything we can. And as Ankur puts it, when you fight with a good problem, it fights back. That fight is where understanding comes from.
