Part two. What AI actually solved. Start with the prime numbers, the atoms of multiplication. Every whole number is built from them, but their pattern has always been a mystery. Bernhard Riemann found a function that works like signal processing for the primes. Alex compares it to a microphone turning your voice into frequencies. The Riemann hypothesis says that in the music of the primes, every instrument plays at exactly the same volume. In math terms: all the important zeros of this function sit on one single line, the line one half.

For seventy years, the best anyone could prove was a zero free zone that got thinner and thinner. The AI proved an entire strip with no zeros at all, from seven eighths to one. Push that seven eighths down to one half, and you have the full Riemann hypothesis. Alex is careful here. This method fundamentally can't get there. A new idea is needed. But the proof is checked in Lean, so he is a hundred percent sure it's correct.

Then Navier-Stokes, the equations behind every fluid on Earth. They are basically F equals m a for water and air. The million dollar question: can a smooth flow suddenly blow up to infinity? University of Washington engineer Steve Brunton explains what the AI found for one version of the problem. A perfectly orchestrated vortex that keeps stretching and thinning, like a figure skater pulling in their arms, spinning faster and faster until, at one point, it becomes infinite. But here's the twist. Steve says almost nothing changed for engineers. Airplane wings and artificial hearts are just as hard to simulate as before. We solved the million dollar math problem, he says. The trillion dollar engineering problem is still open.

And the Unique Games Conjecture? It's a bet about which hard problems we can even approximate. If it's true, it organizes a huge part of computer science. Ankur admits he was one of the doubters. It sounded too nice to be true. It's true.
