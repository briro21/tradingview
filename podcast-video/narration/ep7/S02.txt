Part two. The escalation trap.

An escalation trap, Pape explains, is when a strong country attacks a weaker one and wins tactically. The bombs hit their targets. Leaders are killed. And yet the weaker country gains power. In this war, he says, Iran quickly tightened its grip on the Strait of Hormuz, and with it, about twenty percent of the world's oil.

Most people, Pape says, think of strategy as chess. White takes a pawn, looks ahead, then loses a bishop. In chess, the sensible move is to offer a draw. That's what everyone keeps telling the president: just take a deal.

But Pape says international politics isn't chess. It's roulette.

You bet on red. It comes up black. A rational player walks away. But the gambler doubles down. Since red didn't come up, surely it will next time. It comes up black again. Now he's way behind, so he borrows chips from a bookie and bets even bigger.

Pape maps each spin onto the war. Bet one: kill the Supreme Leader, and Iran will weaken. It didn't, he says. Bet two: a six week air campaign. Iran kept the strait. Bet three: a blockade. Six months later, Iran hasn't collapsed and gas prices keep rising.

And the chips? American gas prices, American lives, American interest rates. The borrowed chips are the party's support. And the bookie wants them back on January third, when the new Congress is seated.

So why not just stop? Because, Pape says, presidents don't walk away from their legacy. Lyndon Johnson played the same game in Vietnam, chasing his losses up the escalation ladder. That pressure of humiliation, he warns, pushes the loser to keep doubling down. Which brings us to the plan on the president's desk.
