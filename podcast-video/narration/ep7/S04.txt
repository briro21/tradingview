Part four. Putin's next move, and a wildfire at home.

Now, the second crisis. What does Putin actually want? Pape pulls up the map. Russia has formally annexed four regions of Ukraine, but still doesn't control all of them. After four years and, by the most conservative count, at least two hundred and fifty thousand Russian dead, he says Putin has gained only about one percent more of Ukraine.

So Putin doesn't want a photo op with Trump. He wants Trump to deliver territory. And Pape doesn't think Trump can. Only Ukraine can give it up, and he believes Zelensky would be risking his life if he did.

So around Thanksgiving, Pape expects Putin to move to his next plan: a dark winter. Strike the power stations in Kyiv, Kharkiv and Odesa, aiming to knock out ninety percent of the power for a month or two. Because Putin, he says, is in his own escalation trap. Accepting defeat could cost him not just his legacy, but his life.

Meanwhile, back in America, the third crisis: what Pape calls violent populism. That's when political violence moves from the fringe to the mainstream.

He uses a wildfire analogy. In fire season, you can predict how much dry wood is lying around. What you can't predict is the spark. The dry wood is the tens of millions of Americans who support political violence. The spark can be anything: a single post, a clash between federal agents and protesters over ballot counting.

And then the crises start to feed each other. Pape points to riots in France that spread to other cities, and then beyond France. A big part of it, he says, was rising prices from the Iran war. Higher gas, higher diesel, a frozen housing market. Three crises, no longer separate.
