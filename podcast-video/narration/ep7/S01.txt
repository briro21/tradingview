Part one. The eight most dangerous weeks.

Robert Pape has studied political violence around the world for thirty years. The last five, he says, he has spent on one country: the United States.

His starting point is the midterm elections. Americans elect a president every four years, but Congress every two. When the vote falls in the middle of a president's term, it's called a midterm. Right now, Pape notes, the president's party controls both the House and the Senate, which means very little check on the president. Lose them, and that changes overnight.

But Pape's real worry isn't the vote. It's what comes after. Counting, certifying, and seating the winners takes about two months. And in his view, the transfer of power is the danger zone for political violence. We saw it on January sixth, which, he points out, came at the end of exactly this kind of two month process.

Then he shows the number that keeps him up at night. In his latest national survey, over forty million Americans support using the military against protesters who oppose the Trump agenda. And over sixty million support using force to stop Trump from being president. Violence on both sides of the aisle. Pape says these numbers have doubled since September twenty twenty four.

And what if Democrats win? Subpoenas. Impeachments, and not just of the president, but of cabinet secretaries. In Pape's words, there is no political bottom there.

But here's the thing. This isn't happening in isolation. It's happening in the middle of a war with Iran. And at the same time, Russia is threatening Ukraine's power grid. Three crises, the same eight weeks. To see why they might feed each other, you first need Pape's most important idea.
