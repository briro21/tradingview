Part two. The subsidy.

To understand AI's economics, Zitron says, you need to understand tokens. A token is roughly three quarters of a word. AI companies charge for them like a taxi meter: so much per million tokens going in, so much per million coming out. Even the model's "thinking" runs up the meter.

But on a monthly plan, you never see the meter. And this is where it gets interesting. Zitron cites an analysis by the research firm SemiAnalysis: on a two hundred dollar a month ChatGPT plan, a heavy user can burn through around fourteen thousand dollars worth of tokens. On Anthropic's plan, around eight thousand. Even the twenty dollar plan, about four hundred.

That, he argues, is why the losses are so big. OpenAI lost twenty point nine billion dollars last year, because people can use as much as they want.

Then came the test. When companies were moved onto paying the real per token price, Zitron says, the mood changed fast. His example: Uber burned through its entire annual token budget in three months. Suddenly, the most productive tool ever became a cost to control.

His question is simple. If people paid what AI actually costs, would they still use it this much?

Steven Bartlett pushes back. Tech companies have always lost money to grab market share, and costs usually fall. Zitron's answer: if the costs were coming down, we'd see it. Instead, he says, it keeps getting more expensive, and even the companies renting out chips don't seem to be profitable.

So if it's this expensive, why is everyone using it? That's where the conversation gets heated.
