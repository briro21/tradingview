Part six. What would prove him wrong?

Remember the question from the start? Bartlett asks it directly. What would it take for you to be wrong?

Zitron's answer: a hardware breakthrough that cuts the cost of AI by something like a thousand times. A product that works on its own, reliably, without the complicated setup. In his words, it would have to be "indistinguishable from magic." And he says the bar is that high because the companies set it there themselves.

So what should ordinary people do? Zitron is careful. He says he isn't giving financial advice, and that he personally keeps his money in cash. His advice is about attitude: be suspicious of tech promises. Watch the wording. When a company reports an "annualized run rate," he says, ask what that actually means, because they rarely define it.

And one piece of advice he says he lives by: every con begins by rushing you. Unless something is truly on fire, slow down.

Bartlett ends with balance. He says he doesn't fully agree, but that voices like Zitron's force him to think harder, and that the best approach is to collect many perspectives and follow the evidence yourself.

Zitron's last words aren't about money at all. He says the best part of being a critic has been the people around him. So tell the people you love that you love them. Tell the writers and creators you enjoy that their work matters.
