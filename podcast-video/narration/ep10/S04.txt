Part four. Is the music still playing?

Elson borrows a line from the film Margin Call. On Wall Street, the job is to listen to the music, and to know when it's about to stop.

His read: the music is still playing. Meta's twenty-five billion dollar stock sale was five times oversubscribed. An Amazon bond deal drew more than sixty billion dollars of orders. Microsoft pulled off one of the most sought-after debt offerings on record. Investors still trust companies with real cash flow.

But the notes are going out of tune. Bond yields at CoreWeave have soared. OpenAI cut its funding target. And now Anthropic's IPO has shrunk. To Elson, it's the clearest signal yet that the music is getting quieter.

The bigger shift, he says, is that big tech used to pay for its ambitions out of its own profits. Now it needs outside money. He points to nearly a trillion dollars of planned debt from the big cloud companies by twenty twenty-eight. Meta alone, he notes, has a sixty billion dollar equity raise planned for next year.

Zitron puts it in one line. The stock market measures how excited you should be. The bond market measures how scared you should be. And he notes reports that Anthropic could get a below investment grade rating on its debt, which would shut out many pension and investment funds.

And venture capital? Asked what it measures, Zitron's answer is not polite. Private investors, both men agree, were the easiest crowd of all.

Which brings us to the company that leaned on that crowd the most.
