Part six. Without Jensen.

This is where Zitron's opening claim comes back. In his view, AI was always a confidence game, and Jensen Huang was its number one salesman. The industry, he says, doesn't just need to stay this big. It needs to keep growing every single year. Without Jensen backstopping deals, he doesn't see anyone who can do that. He points to Elon Musk, who he says is trying to raise forty billion dollars to buy more Nvidia chips. Imagine walking into a bank today, he says, and asking for billions to buy GPUs from that Nvidia.

Nvidia's new chief executive is its former finance chief, Colette Kress. Zitron says she's exactly what you want in a finance chief, careful and dull, but not someone who will keep writing the cheques that held the industry up. He calls this the beginning of the unraveling, and expects a painful reckoning for venture capital.

Now the balance. Elson's view is that for the strongest companies, the music is still playing, and investors are still lending to businesses with real cash flow. Zitron himself says Nvidia is not Enron: real things were built, and real chips were sold. And the cases against Nvidia's leadership are still allegations, not verdicts.

So what should you watch? Three things, from these two conversations. How Anthropic's shares trade after the IPO. Whether lenders keep funding AI debt, and at what price. And whether OpenAI can raise its next round. This is their analysis, not financial advice.

If Zitron is right, the AI boom was never just a technology story. It was a sales story. And the best salesman just left the stage.
