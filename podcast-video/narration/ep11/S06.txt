Part six. Who pays?

Start with gamers. Steve doesn't expect hardware prices to get much better any time soon, and he doesn't see a flood of cheap second-hand parts coming, because these chips and long-term contracts were never built for ordinary computers.

Then communities. Steve describes a data center his team visited for a documentary. It received seventy-seven million dollars in tax breaks, in a relatively poor area, and has fewer than twenty employees. Local hires, he says, mostly work in security, landscaping and cleaning. Zitron adds that once construction ends, many of these sites need only around fifty or sixty people. And Steve mentions the planned West Virginia site, which he says may clear one of the few remaining chestnut-producing forests in the country. The fallout, he says, lands mostly on the people it always lands on. Not the billionaires.

And the economy. Zitron argues that the idea that buying more chips grows the economy has been oversold. He says an economist he spoke with agreed that the way official statistics adjust software prices may have overstated software's contribution to growth since twenty twenty-two. That is his argument, not a settled finding.

So what should you watch? Four things. Whether Nvidia files its quarterly report by December fourth. Whether Anthropic's next quarter clears the twenty-one billion dollar bar Zitron mentions. Whether OpenAI can raise money early next year. And whether any of the neoclouds runs out of road.

This is Zitron's analysis, not financial advice. Nvidia still posted record numbers, and Anthropic's shares are above their listing price. Zitron's view is that those numbers depend on a confidence game, and that the person who ran it is gone.
