Part five. The death watch.

Zitron's sharpest prediction is about the so-called neoclouds. These are companies that raise debt to buy Nvidia chips, using those same chips as collateral, then rent the computing power to a small group of AI customers. Often the same customers Nvidia itself has invested in.

He has put two of them on what he calls a death watch. The first is Nscale, which, he says, recently told investors it needs to raise more money than expected, and which is still negotiating backing for a forty-five billion dollar data center project for Anthropic in West Virginia. The second is Firmus, an Australian data center company that cut its IPO and then pulled it. And Lambda, another chip renter, is hoping to go public next year, which only works if investors still trust the sector.

His point is about confidence. Banks lent to these companies, he argues, because they believed Nvidia would step in if anything went wrong. Without that implicit guarantee, a bank asks a simple question: is Nvidia backing this? And if the answer is we're still waiting to hear, the money doesn't come.

It doesn't need all of them to fail, he says. One insolvency could make lenders ask whether the whole sector is just debt stacked on the same chips and the same customers.

His guest, Steve from the hardware channel Gamers Nexus, adds a fair counterpoint. Blaming everything on one man isn't quite right, he says. They were all part of this together.

So what does any of this mean for people who never bought a single share?
