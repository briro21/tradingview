Part five. The garbage-bag playbook.

Cuban's father did car upholstery, and never made more than forty thousand dollars, as Bartlett puts it. At twelve, Mark wanted new basketball shoes. His dad said: get a job. A friend at the poker table offered him boxes of garbage bags for three dollars, to sell for six. Mark went door to door. If you can sell door to door, he says, you can do anything.

Later, in Dallas, he shared a three-bedroom apartment with five other guys and slept on the floor. He got fired from a software store for leaving to pick up a commission check. So he started his own company, Micro Solutions.

His edge? With any new software, he figured only the person who wrote it would understand it better than him. Everyone else would be tied for second, and he would outwork them. He read the manuals through the night. He says it's exactly the same with AI today.

And that company was profitable every single month for eight years. That's his warning to young founders. Raising money and chasing big sales numbers isn't the goal. Staying alive is. And he's blunt about who should even try. If you have to ask yourself whether you can be an entrepreneur, he says, you probably aren't one.

He sold Micro Solutions, then built Broadcast dot com, which Yahoo bought for five point seven billion dollars in stock.

For anyone stuck in a job, his advice is simple. Don't hunt for the perfect job. Just get a job, and get really good at it. Be brutally honest about what you're bad at. And ignore the get-rich-quick videos in your feed. His line: nobody who has an edge in the stock market is selling it. They're using it.

Which brings us back to that crash.
