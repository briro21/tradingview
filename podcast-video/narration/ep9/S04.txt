Part four. Twenty-five agents on his phone.

Here's the contradiction that isn't one. Cuban calls himself the biggest proponent of AI there is. He's been in tech for decades, and he says this dwarfs everything before it.

He runs about twenty-five AI agents that handle the tedious work for him. Because of them, he says, he finally read a non-fiction book for the first time in twenty-five years.

One real example. At Cost Plus Drugs, his company that sells cheaper prescription medicine, he's fighting what he calls the big healthcare conglomerates. Now an agent tells him every time one of them gets sued, and every time they bid on a contract. It even drafts the public records requests to get copies. Before, he'd have had to hire, train and manage someone. So it never got done.

Bartlett catches the obvious problem. Aren't those tasks someone would have been paid to do? Cuban admits it. He might once have hired an intern at fifteen or twenty dollars an hour. His argument is that the time saved creates new ideas, and new businesses that hire different people.

He calls it creative destruction. He went to college near Terre Haute, Indiana, where RCA once made records, then CDs, then DVDs. Cassettes are gone. Money in music isn't. But he's honest that the path will be painful for a lot of people.

His advice to his own kids is blunt. Learn agents. Anyone who does will be far more productive than anyone who doesn't. And the edge isn't the information. It's knowing which questions to ask, and judging four different answers.

Which brings us to how Cuban built his own edge.
