Part six. Where the real money is made.

Cuban borrows from Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, who were obsessed with compounding, and who sometimes went years without investing at all. With AI stocks going to the moon on a mountain of debt, his advice is patience. Invest a little for fun if you want, but keep some cash. Because at some point the market turns, and that, he says, is where the real money is made.

He's done it before. When the market crashed during Covid, he says he started buying up everything, and it paid off.

So how is he playing defense now? He says he has money spread across more than one country, and he's invested far less than usual. The old Buffett move: wait and see. He admits he might lose a little by being too careful.

To be clear, this is Cuban's view, not financial advice.

So what would change his mind about AI? His answer: show him an AI that can solve hard problems with minimal resources, like a brilliant twelve-year-old with nothing but curiosity. As long as AI needs more chips and more power than ever before, he says, that's not intelligence. It's brute force. He's just as skeptical of the biggest promises. Elon Musk, he says, is the best entrepreneur of our lifetimes, but he's also a sales guy who has to raise a lot of money.

And even the experts can't agree. Bartlett has interviewed AI safety researchers and people inside the big labs, and Cuban notes that no two of them give the same answer.

Bartlett closes not on markets but on character. He says Cuban shows you can be very successful and still be a good, happy person. Cuban's own lesson: if you're miserable when you're poor, money won't fix it.
