The deepest human fear is death. The fear that we will simply disappear from the universe. But if reality as we know it is a model built by the brain, then the self you cling to is part of that model. Your identity is not a physical object. Bach describes it as a representation, a pattern that your brain creates to keep track of what it is doing.

Software does not breathe. It has no fixed body. In that sense, Bach argues, the self is not the kind of thing that is born or dies the way a body is. What ends at death is the process that keeps telling the story.

He gives a fascinating example: the Dalai Lama. In Tibetan tradition, the Dalai Lama is not just one man. It is a role, an identity, that is deliberately passed on to the next person, generation after generation. A kind of software that is consciously reinstalled into a new body. And Bach suggests that all of us work a little like this. You are not your cells. You are a story your organism tells about itself.

From this point of view, the fear of death is the panic of a program that realizes it will be shut down before its job is done. It is a useful panic. It keeps the primate alive. But it is not the final truth about who you are.

Bach also points out that through meditation, or through a radical shift of attention, people can sometimes see this mechanism directly. They notice that the ego is a tool, a control model the body uses, and not the master of the house. Seeing that can be frightening. It can also be strangely liberating.
