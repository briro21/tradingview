We wake up every day, go to work, and feel that this routine is absolutely real. But in his conversation with Lex Fridman, Joscha Bach describes our existence in a way that is hard to unsee. His claim is simple and unsettling: you have never actually lived in the physical world.

Think about where your brain is. It sits in total darkness, sealed inside your skull. It cannot see, hear, or touch anything directly. All it ever receives are electrical signals, spikes travelling along nerves. So where does the world come from? The colors, the sounds, the geometry of the room around you?

Bach's answer is that your brain builds it. Everything you see is a kind of virtual reality, a model generated to explain those signals. Your brain runs something like the physics engine of a video game, a fast, simplified simulation of objects, gravity, faces and space. It is not a perfect copy of the outside world. It is a useful story that lets a primate survive.

And the physical world itself? Bach suggests it is something very different from what we perceive: a vast pattern of quantum mechanical processes, too fine and too fast to ever experience directly. What we call reality is the dream that our nervous system tells about it. You are not in the world. You are in a multimedia novel that your brain keeps writing, moment by moment.
