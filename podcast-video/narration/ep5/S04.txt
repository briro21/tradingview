What is life, really? Bach starts with the cell. A cell is an extraordinary molecular machine. It harvests energy from its environment and uses it to keep its own structure in order, pushing back against entropy. In a sense, a cell is a biological computer, running a program that keeps it alive.

When billions of cells come together to form a plant, an animal or a human, they need rules to cooperate. Something has to coordinate them, so that they act as one organism and not as a crowd. And here Bach makes one of his most surprising claims.

He says that when ancient cultures talked about spirit, they were pointing at something real. Spirit is an old word for what we would now call an operating system. It is the software that runs an autonomous robot, the organizing pattern that makes a body act as one. A plant has something like a spirit that links its cells. An ecosystem has one. You have one.

And it does not stop at the individual. Families, companies, religions and nations are higher level operating systems. They coordinate millions of people, the way your mind coordinates billions of cells. A beehive works like this. So does a civilization. We like to think of ourselves as independent heroes, but in many ways we are cells inside a superorganism. And a superorganism does not care about any single cell. It cares about what keeps the whole system running.

Think about how strange that is. The beliefs you defend, the rules you follow without thinking, even the things you find beautiful. Many of them were installed by the larger system you were born into.

That raises the most personal question of all. If you are software running on a primate body, what happens to you when the body stops?
