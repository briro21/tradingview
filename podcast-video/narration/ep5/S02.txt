Since the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution, we have felt like gods. But look at the price. In roughly a century, we have burned through fossil fuels that took the earth a hundred million years to store. Trees and ancient life, compressed into carbon, set on fire in the blink of an eye.

For thousands of years, the human population stayed in the hundreds of millions. Then it exploded into billions, because we found a way to borrow energy from the deep past. Bach describes our situation with a striking image: we are skiing down the slope of an entropic abyss. We are going fast, it feels exhilarating, and almost nobody is asking how the run ends.

Look at your own life. You wake up in an air conditioned room, drive an air conditioned car, and walk into an air conditioned office. It is a closed cooling chain. Our food travels the same way, in refrigerated trucks, to refrigerated shelves. If that chain breaks for a few days in a brutal heat wave, many people will not survive. We are far more fragile than we feel. Our great grandparents knew how to grow food, mend clothes and survive a hard winter. Most of us would not last a week without supply chains we never even see.

In nature, a species that consumes its environment faster than it can recover behaves like a parasite, and it usually collapses. Our civilization is betting that we can stay in control of this machine. But in Bach's view, it is not clear that anyone is steering. The machine is rolling downhill, and no one knows which button would stop it.

So could something smarter than us pull the emergency brake? Many people hope that something is artificial intelligence.
